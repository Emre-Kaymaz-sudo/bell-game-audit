# Novelty audit and scope of the revised project

**Search date: 23 November 2026.** The original engine is a reproducible instance of established Bell-game design. The revised contribution is a referee audit. It holds a game's one-round question frequencies fixed, changes how questions are scheduled and when their history is revealed, and calculates the corresponding local benchmark. We found prior work on each underlying mechanism. In the sources and code searched we did not locate the same combination of exact delayed-feedback benchmarks, private-history reference solutions, and an audit of an inverse-designed game. Scientific priority remains open.

## Exact questions the code answers

The score is the arithmetic mean of the individual round wins. A uniform shuffle of a specified multiset is independent of the players, who may agree on a strategy and share randomness before the block. Each receives only their current local question. Earlier answers and win/loss feedback are hidden from the other player. Current answers never affect future questions or announcements. A fixed memoryless quantum strategy retains its original expected score because the marginal question law at each position stays fixed.

**Public histories.** At a given round let the remaining pair quotas be r, with k cards left. Enumerate all deterministic local tables and take the maximum number of winning cards in r, then divide by k. Both players can agree to use that maximizing table when the history identifies r. To obtain the exact positional expectation, average over r with weights equal to the product of the per-cell binomial coefficients divided by C(N,k). The order is independent of past answers, so choosing an optimal table for each state simultaneously attains the mean. The output uses rational arithmetic. [Public solver](bell_game_audit/audit/quota.py)

**Private or delayed histories.** For each round and already public prefix, the two questions of a larger XOR game are the remaining local input prefixes available to Alice and Bob. Weight each prefix pair by its number of possible schedule completions. Enumerating the response tables yields its exact local score. Different rounds can be optimized independently because outputs do not alter future information. The reference solver has explicit size limits for this exponential calculation. [Tiny-block solver](bell_game_audit/audit/private_history.py)

### Why private histories preserve the one-round value on a uniform rectangle

Fix a round t. Suppose the deck contains the same positive number of copies of every input pair on a complete rectangle. Using only shared randomness, the two players can generate a reference shuffle conditional on its t-th card being (0,0). Given their actual current questions (x,y), Alice cyclically shifts all her reference input labels by x and Bob shifts all his by y in their respective alphabets. These separate relabelings give a bijection from shuffles with (0,0) at t to shuffles with (x,y) at t. Their synthetic prefixes therefore have the exact joint conditional law of the real private prefixes. Any private-history classical strategy at t can be simulated in the one-round game. Repeating a best one-round table attains the bound. The argument also allows private memory of one's own past outputs, which a deterministic strategy computes from its local inputs.

For **arbitrary CHSH quotas**, write N for the deck size and m for its smallest cell count. Identify a subdeck with m copies of each of the four cards. Give the players its positions and the entire order of the remainder as extra information. Conditional on that information, the balanced subdeck's shuffle is independent, so at most three quarters of its 4m positions can be won in expectation. Even granting a win at every other position gives a block ceiling of (N−m)/N. A static table losing only at a least frequent pair attains this value. The m=0 case gives 1. This derivation concerns private histories and expected wins. Paper-level priority for the derivation has not been established.

### A checkable bound for the 27-round game

A four-card rectangle with odd target parity is a relabeled CHSH subdeck. In a uniform shuffle of the full deck, each such subdeck loses at least one round **in expectation** with private histories. To bound its contribution, one may reveal its positions and every other card's order. Its own internal order remains independent. Summing the loss bounds over disjoint subdecks gives a certificate. This argument extends the familiar disjoint-frustrated-cycle bound to this specific information protocol.

The first certificate used three disjoint four-card rectangles. It gave the interval **[22/27, 24/27]**. A stronger certificate now establishes **22/27 exactly** using one nine-card rectangle and three six-card cycles. For immediate public feedback the exact reachable value is **492544354319/542115674100**, or **90.85595%**. The fixed quantum strategy retains a positive modeled margin even in the weakest calibration profile under the private protocol. The public benchmark exceeds each of its three modeled scores. ### Reference cases and remaining scope

The four-card CHSH block tests all 24 possible orderings. A one-round delayed announcement gives 7/8, a two-round delay gives 13/16, and withholding joint announcements throughout the block gives 3/4. As soon as a past card becomes public, a strategy can put its sole losing pair on that exhausted card. Before the first announcement the private-history proof applies. These values are independent cross-checks for the implementation.

The new packing and local-relabeling construction closes the private-history interval throughout the specified 27-card grid and for its positive integer multiples. Open questions include exact intermediate disclosure delays for the weighted winner, the distribution of total wins in a realized block, and the value of adaptive entangled strategies. This computational result makes no priority claim for sharply transitive actions or signed-cycle bounds.
